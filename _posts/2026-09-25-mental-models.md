I was recently listening to the latest episode of [Jane Street's podcast](https://signalsandthreads.com/learning-goals-and-the-goals-of-learning-teaching-in-the-age-of-ai/), and there was much discussion on the topic of mental models. As a college student, my full-time job is to *learn*. As such, I am interested in the practice of meta-learning, or "learning to learn". This blog post is meant to crystallize some of my thoughts on mental models, and is itself an experiment in learning.

In any field, it is impossible to understand any and every aspect of it. As humans, it is impossible to hold the sheer magnitude of information that is apparent in every field. Because of this, it is necessary to develop mental models. These mental models provide an abstracted, and most often simplified, version of the system or mechanism we are trying to understand. This abstraction keeps our minds from being overloaded and enables thinking big-picture. When learning, it is important to strike a balance between the abstracting concepts without completely black-boxing the internals of a system.

This is something I believe math courses do very well. A lecture generally consists of the professor lecturing at the blackboard, writing definitions and proving theorems by hand. Afterwards, if you should need to use the theorem, it is usually accepted to cite the result without having to re-derive it yourself. The initial effort spent proving the theorem refines the mental model of the student and can also give clues as to how it can applied in different situations. 

Writing software is very similar to the process of learning math. It is true that abstraction is one of the core principles of good software engineering, as it would be completely impractical if you needed to write every program manually into machine instructions that run on the bare processor. Modern programming is built on top of an endless number of libraries and technologies that have been developed by many brilliant programmers over many years. For a while, programming had that same balance of abstraction and understanding that mathematics has, but with the advent of incredibly capable coding agents, the landscape has completely shifted. Nowadays, it is much easier to reach for an LLM to quickly understand a new library instead of digging through cryptic API docs trying to find a function that does the thing you are looking for. As a result, programmers are pumping out code faster than ever. In theory, this should be great. Coding is democratized, and those who are proficient can ship products at a rate that 10 years ago could only be achieved by the mythical 10x dev. However, in offloading the programming, I believe we are sacrificing understanding. Young programmers are putting more and more trust in these models, often writing software that they simply don't understand. In saying this, I also admit that I am no exception. It is all too easy to prompt a model to create a program that looks plausibly correct, runs without crashing, and then convince yourself of a job well done.

This brings me back to the point of mental models. In order to adapt to this new paradigm of programming, we must develop different and more robust mental models. I'm not entirely certain what this entails, but I am almost certain that the way we think about software will change drastically over the next couple years. In the meantime, I believe it is important to stay flexible and continue to learn how to learn hard things. Then if ASI really does bring about mass job displacement, you'll hopefully be in a better position to reposition and refocus onto something new.
