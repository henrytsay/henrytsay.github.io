As I progress further in my college career and creep closer towards graduation, I have spent a lot of time thinking about how best to reach my goals. And oftentimes the answer is to simply "get better." Get better at coding, get better at reading, get better at research, get better at problem solving. But what does it really mean to "get better," and how can I do it? In answering this question, I think it would be fruitful to look towards the model of musicians. To begin, I will outline a few core principles of musical practice that can be applied to academic learning.

## Bench time

One class of problems in music can be solved by what Leon Fleisher calls "bench time." This refers to the amount of time actively spent at the physical (piano) bench working on technique like scales, arpeggios, and challenging passages. This is a non-optional but straightforward part of practice. Simply putting the hours in is what is necessary to solve these problems. In academics, this corresponds to the time you spend memorizing facts, solving (variations of) problems you've seen before, and just generally getting comfortable with remembering and applying knowledge that may not necessarily challenge you. This is what I find myself most often working on because it is clear what needs to be done, but it is not by itself sufficient to improve meaningfully. For many, this is the only type of practice they engage in, but I believe that is a mistake. The following sections provide a much more nuanced view of practice and its benefits.

## Building taste

"Get the sound you want in your head first, then play it." Bud Herseth was the former principal trumpet of the Chicago Symphony Orchestra and one of the greatest brass players to ever live. There are stories of people asking him how he is able to produce a beautiful tone, to which his answer was that he imagines such an exquisite sound in his head that if he is able to capture even a fraction of his imagined sound in his real playing that it would sound heavenly to most. Developing this internal sound is done by listening to incredible players as much as possible. There can be a tendency to optimize the process of listening by overanalyzing each and every aspect of a player's sound, but I have found that this is the wrong approach. In my own experience, I have found that the process of exposing yourself to a great sound over and over again is enough to develop your mind's ideal sound. After all, it makes sense that if you listen to nothing but beautiful music, your expectations will gradually shift towards that higher standard. Taste can be learned, but it is ultimately something that works on intuition rather than deep and rational thought. The academic analog here is the use of [mental models]({% post_url 2026-09-25-mental-models %}). Just as I focus on the sound and let my body handle the mechanics, in problem-solving we must focus on developing robust mental models before we even begin to look for a solution. Everything must be approached from an understanding-first perspective, rather than rote memorization. In order to develop academic taste, it is necessary to read great proofs, well-written papers, insightful blogs, and elegant code. Critically engaging with these media is how you begin to understand how great minds think. As you read, challenge, and digest good work, you will be begin to develop your own sense of taste. It is this distinguishing taste that separates the excellent from the mediocre. 

## Experimentation

As a musician, the practice room is a safe space. This does not mean that I have not had my fair share of struggles and emotional strife in a practice room, but rather that it is a place free of any expectations and a place where I am unafraid to fail. This gives me the freedom to experiment with my own playing, seeing what works and what doesn't. Adding crescendos, altering dynamics, or taking extra rubato in a passage are all things that I may try in the music I'm working on. If I like the result, I'll spend time solidifying the idea for the future. If it doesn't work out, then I continue on and try something else. The important thing to remember here is that the practice room is meant for this type of experimentation. While it's nice when all the choices you make turn out to be successes, we must remember that failures are just as informative. To go back to the point of developing taste, it is important to both understand what you like as well as what you don't. In constructing an "academic practice room," we need to give ourselves the same creative freedom. If you're writing a paper, this means getting a messy first-draft down on the page. If you're writing a proof, then begin scrawling down whatever approaches and ideas come to mind regardless of whether you think they'll work. This is the experimentation phase in the practice room that you can then begin to refine into something more polished and performance-ready. Another trick musicians often use in their practice is to augment the written music. For example, there is a famous trombone excerpt in the Ride of the Valkyries that is built upon the core rhythm of a dotted-eighth, sixteenth, and then an eighth note. In practice, a player may play the entire rhythm as 3 successive eighth notes, or even invert the dotted-eighth and sixteenth. The idea behind this is that after playing an excerpt so many times, it is easy to get stuck in habits you didn't even know you'd developed. Altering the rhythm forces you to think harder about the music, and thus helps even when you return to playing what was originally written. A similar technique is also used in math. After reading a proof, it is helpful to perturb the questions in ways that challenge your understanding. "What if this assumption were false? What if we added/relaxed this restriction? Is this true generally?" By considering these alternate situations, we ensure that our minds stay flexible and ready to reason about any situation.

## Mindful practice

In contrast to the idea of "bench time," musicians are always talking about mindful practice. Mindful practice means that you don't just spend your valuable practice time mindlessly playing a piece down from start to finish. You must go in with a plan. If you've struggled with a quick jump in rehearsal, then you isolate those specific bars and you work on it until you can do it in your sleep. If you notice your sound isn't matching your mental ideal, then you take out a tuner and work on long tones. Time is valuable, and you should strive to approach every minute of your practice with a clear purpose and intention. This is the dedicated approach that professional musicians follow, and it is the secret to their success. One thing that I have always found striking is the amount of time that musicians spend practicing. If you ask a professional musician how often they practice, it'll likely range from 1-3 hours a day. Chopin himself advised his students not to practice more than 2-3 hours daily because he believed it was excessive and in some cases, harmful. 3 hours may initially sound like a lot, but when we consider that kids go to school for over 8 hours and most people spend more time on their phones a day, 3 hours doesn't seem all that much. In modern society, people have begun to conflate time spent with meaningful progress. If you search on YouTube, you can find dozens of livestreams of students studying for 8 hours straight. A typical 9-5 knowledge-work job consists of 8-hour work days. What these people (and corporations) fail to realize is the old cliché of quality over quantity. This is where I believe that Cal Newport gets it right with his [*Deep Work*](https://calnewport.com/writing/) philosphy. The fact that professional musicians are able to get to the top of their field with 3 hours of focused, deliberate practice suggests that taking a similar approach to academic development will yield more meaningful intellectual growth in less time. Concretely, this can be broken down into a number of features:

1. **Practice in parts**. The first thing I do whenever I begin work on a new piano piece is take a pencil and mark every phrase and label each section. If two sections are the same or similar, then I know that I can just practice one and both parts will improve. If I struggle with a particular phrase, then I can focus on that. When learning an academic subject, we must deconstruct the skill in the same way. If you're looking to improve your algorithmic problem-solving skills, isolate the types of problems you struggle on (ex. dynamic programming) and focus your efforts there.
2. **Tight feedback loops**. In the practice room, feedback is instant. Music is subjective, which is why it is important to develop taste, but it is still immediately clear whether or not the sound that comes out of your instrument is what you wanted. With that information, you can quickly adjust and try again, creating the fast iteration loop necessary for improvement. In academics, this is much harder. It's difficult to tell whether you're succeeding in a class until you take an exam, and even then you only get feedback weeks later when the exam is returned. It might take so long for the results of a technical job interview to get back to you that you don't even remember what you were asked. Because of this, it is important to create scenarios where you can force instant feedback. When learning something new, periodically stop and test yourself. This could mean sitting down with a blank sheet of paper and pencil and drawing out a mental model or quizzing yourself on key concepts. Taking the time to evaluate your own performance is well worth it, as your brain is able to immediately correct itself and improve its own mental model.
3. **Practice the hard things**. Practicing should not feel comfortable or easy all the time. Mindful practice requires intense concentration as you work to expand your capabilities further than before. The mental load of this depth of concentration is why musicians limit their practice. Ensure that you pick problems and learn things that truly challenge you, because that is where there is the most growth to be had.
4. **Rest**. As a trombone player, it simply isn't feasible to practice for 3 hours straight due to the physical demands of the instrument. Because of this, I find it best to break down my practice into multiple, smaller blocks of focused practice. This serves two purposes, as it gives my chops a chance to rest and allows my brain to consolidate the ideas I worked on in the practice session. If you approach academic learning with the principle of mindful practice, it also requires just as much rest. Meaningfully deep work is tiring, and your brain must be given adequate time to recuperate between study sessions.

## Fulfillment

I began this post by motivating this approach to practice as a way to "get better" at a number of things I believe necessary for a fruitful career. This is a large part of it, but not the only reason why I am interested in this practice. In high school, there was a period of time in which I had completely dedicated myself to the trombone in order to prepare for an audition for a youth orchestra. I'd spend hours a day practicing, even going so far as to skip lunches just so I could get another 30 minutes of playing. If I was in a particularly boring class, I'd be running through music in my head, imagining my slide arm moving back and forth as I played an imaginary horn. If I wasn't playing the trombone, then I was listening to it: bathing myself in the luxurious sound of the CSO brass or the extroverted playing of Christian Lindberg. During this time, I rapidly improved. I ultimately achieved my desired outcome, but more than that, I realized this was one of the happiest times of my life. Being motivated so deeply by a goal and pursuing it with dedicated practice and determination is the greatest feeling in life. The lessons I have learned as a musician have shaped the way that I approach everything in my life, and I believe the principles of practice outlined here can help anyone achieve that same level of fulfillment that comes from deep work.

As much as this is a guide for the reader, it is also a guide for myself. I strive to approach my academic career with the same passion as a musician, and that path begins with practice. This blog post provides a way to crystallize my knowledge so that it can be better applied to my daily life.
