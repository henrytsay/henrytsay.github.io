In the spirit of writing a blog, I am going to take ideas from fields I know about and extrapolate to subjects that I have absolutely no authority to speak on. As such, it is important to take what I say here with a spoonful of salt, as there is no real evidence or reason to believe that this is actually true.

The basis of all modern large language models (LLMs) is a mechanism called attention. In short, attention allows each word (or more accurately, token) in a given text to *attend* to every other word. In language, it is clearly true that the context within which a word is embedded can give it drastically different meanings. With attention, these models are able to update their internal understanding of the meaning of each word using their relationships with each other. Another way to think about attention is *how important* a given word is to the meaning of the current word. An adjective like "blue" might only affect the noun directly following it, while a single word like "not" could negate the meaning of an entire sentence.

With attention comes the concept of a context window. The context window of a model refers to the number of tokens that can be used in the generation of an output. This is an important parameter, as it controls how much information the model is able to "remember" from its context (e.g. previous conversations, attached documents). As models have improved, so have their context windows. Most frontier models are now capable of handling a context window of 1 million tokens, which is about 750,000 words. However, research has shown that as the model must handle more and more tokens in its context, it can lead to a phenomenon called "[context rot](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)," in which the model's performance degrades as input sizes increase.

With the exposition out of the way, I would like to shift my focus to human attention and what I like to call the "Context-Window Theory of Attention." In the modern age, it is so easy to become inundated with information. At any given moment, we simultaneously have the entirety of the world's knowledge at our fingertips, trillion-dollar companies fighting for our attention to sell ads, and a whole host of other distractions vying for our focus. I think it would be relatively uncontroversial to say that the doomscrolling and consuming of short-form content that many (including me) are so wont to do are unhealthy and not conducive to living a good life. Most people understand that these habits are a net-negative on their life, simply because of how much time they are spending on their phones without really learning and consuming anything meaningful. However, I am asking that we take a step back and consider not only the quality of information we consume, but the *quantity*. My core thesis is that humans, like LLMs, have a limited context window that is maladapted to the modern age.

Every morning, the first thing I do after waking up is check my phone. Whether it's a text message from a friend or a push notification from a news app, it begins the onslaught of information that will continue throughout the day. As I eat breakfast and prepare to go to class, I'll often put in my earbuds and turn on a podcast about politics or some interesting topic in the tech space. The day continues on, and at any given moment it feels as if I need to be consuming some sort of information at all times. If I'm not in lecture listening to my professor, then I'm listening to someone else give a talk on my phone. If I'm sitting on the bus, then I'll open up the New York Times and read articles. It feels as if every single moment of my waking hours is spent consuming as much information as possible. At the end of the day, it feels exhausting. And this differs from the case of doomscrolling, because I am engaging with content that is meaningful and informative. All this content is something I would be happy to dedicate my entire focus to, but the reality is that there is just too little time for that. And while I admit that this is a self-manufactured problem, I believe that it is not an uncommon sentiment. With the internet, there is no limit to the things I feel that I should or need to learn to not fall behind. This feeling is exacerbated by the fact that I am often participating in computer science/AI circles, where everything seems to moving faster and faster. As such, I can't help but feel as if I'm the limiting reactant, that my willpower is the only thing standing between me and a more successful version of myself. And so the logical path would be to begin drinking from the fire hose of information available to me.

But going back to the idea of a finite context window, it is important to remind ourselves that it is simply impossible to manage all that information floating around our minds at all times. If these LLMs suffer from context rot, then it is plausible that we do too. Because of this, it is our job to be incredibly judicious about what we choose to consume. If we only have a certain amount of meaningful space in the context window of our brains, then it may be worthwhile to prioritize the things that truly matter to us. Fittingly, I keep thinking back to the title of the paper that originally introduced the Transformer: "[Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)." Attention is the most meaningful currency of the modern era, both materially (Meta, whose revenue is 98% advertisements, is worth $1.83 trillion at the time of writing this) and intangibly. In machine learning, attention is about *weighing what matters and ignoring the rest*. This idea extends perfectly into our human concept of attention. It is because of this that we must think deeply about how we attend to the world around us.
