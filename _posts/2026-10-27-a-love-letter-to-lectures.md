title: "A Love Letter to Lectures"

As a college student, I find that many of my peers are very skeptical of the benefits of going to lecture. "It's too boring; I'd rather learn it on my own; AI can just teach it to me at my own pace instead." All of these reasons are valid, but their biggest flaw is that they overlook the intangible benefits of attending lecture. It can be easy to view lectures as the straightforward, somewhat boring transfer of knowledge. You attend class, the professor reads off content from the slides, answers questions, and then you leave knowing a couple more concepts that you didn't before.

My contention is that lecture is a form of performance art, just like music or theater. As in these other fields, this requires both excellence and commitment on the part of the lecturer as well as concentration and meaningful engagement from the students. The lecturer must create a compelling narrative that takes the listener along a journey of exposition, discovery, and growth. When this happens, students are able to connect with their teachers on a human level, even without having to hold a one-on-one conversation or attend office hours. On the other side, students must uphold their part of the deal by giving the lecturer their full attention. Lectures, like any art form, take effort to engage with. No matter how good the performer, the performance will fall flat if the audience isn't participating.

There are few things more academically fulfilling than attending a truly good lecture. It's hard to describe what separates a good lecture from a bad one simply because a skilled lecturer is able to perform while making it seem effortless. Instead, I'll talk about what a good lecture *feels* like. As with a great film, I lend my attention to the lecture without forcing myself. Everything the lecturer says feels motivated, and I genuinely care about what they are saying. Each remark has a clear purpose, and I never find myself bored or disengaged enough to pull out my phone or work on something else. One particular example I can recall is my Real Analysis course. Most of my professor's lectures were based on the *Understanding Analysis* textbook by Stephen Abbott (which I think is the best textbook I have ever had the pleasure of reading), but the lecture itself gave even more life to an already incredible piece of expositional writing.

Many people take issues with lectures because they are inherently a one-to-many medium, which means a teacher is not able to account for and adapt their lessons to each individual member of the audience. This is often used as an argument for a future where everyone has a personal AI teacher that completely understands an individual's learning style, matches their pace, and removes the need for lecture-style instruction. However, if we really view lecturing as an art form, this isn't a weakness at all. An orchestra performs for a full concert hall, an author writes a novel that is read by millions around the world, and painters create works that are loved long after their deaths. In all these cases, audiences are able to engage with the work in a deeply personal way. Even as any two viewers may be looking at the same exact painting or listening to the same performance, they can experience wildly different emotions and thoughts. A good lecture is capable of doing the same.

There is also something to be said for the "liveness" of an in-person lecture. As someone who has attended many live orchestra performances, I can say with certainty that there is a real connection between performer and audience that simply cannot be recreated in any recording. In a live lecture, teachers control the shared attention of each student in the room. They can react to confused faces, answer questions, and quickly adapt to the intangible energy of the room. This, of course, is not to say that watching a recorded lecture does not have its merits. There is a lot of good content posted online, and I actually believe that video makes some kinds of lectures possible that couldn't exist live. The best example of this is [3Blue1Brown's animations](https://www.youtube.com/@3blue1brown).

The next time you feel like skipping lecture, try going and taking a moment to appreciate it as a human art form. As with all art, it is invariably true that not everything will speak to you. Regardless, it is beneficial to engage with many lectures, both good and bad. This allows you to form your own opinions on what works and what doesn't, which is an essential step to building your personal taste for communication.
